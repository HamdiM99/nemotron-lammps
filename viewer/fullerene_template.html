A C60 carries charge back and forth between two nanotubes

Two capped (10,10) carbon nanotubes face each other at opposite voltages. A fullerene at the mouth of one tube picks up its charge, is pulled across the gap, bounces off the far cap and comes back with the opposite charge: a nanometre-scale version of Franklin's electrostatic bell. Its charge is recomputed during the flight.

Mechanism

Why it oscillates

  • Charging. While the C60 touches a tube it sits at that tube's potential, so it takes a charge of the same sign.
  • Push and pull. Same sign as its own tube, opposite to the other one: it is repelled from behind and attracted ahead. Above the threshold this beats the van der Waals retention and it leaves.
  • Free flight. In the gap it is an isolated conductor: its charge stays constant. These are the flat steps of the charge curve.
  • Recharging. On touching the other tube it gives up its charge and takes the opposite one: the sharp spike.
  • Return. It runs down the tube, bounces off the cap, comes back to the mouth already carrying the right sign, and leaves again.

The voltage source pays at every transfer, roughly the charge carried times the voltage crossed, and little is dissipated. That is why the charge carried grows from one crossing to the next. Not checked quantitatively.

What the LLM tried on this task

Request in runs 1 and 2: find the lowest voltage that takes the C60 from one tube to the other without breaking it, starting at the mouth. Run 3 was asked to make it oscillate, at least three crossings, with ten flights of up to 30 ps. Four bounded tools, and the verdict of each flight is written by the code, not by the model. The animation at the top plays every one of these flights in order, then the run done by hand.

Run 1: the model answered 14.2 V. That was wrong, and the fault was in the judge: a flight cut off while the C60 was still crossing counted as a failure, and "crossed, intact" did not require the C60 to be stopped in the arrival tube. Run 2, judge hardened: the model read each verdict and changed one parameter at a time, ran out of flights, and reported that it had found no working voltage. It invented no number. Run 3, ten flights and asked for at least three crossings: it found the set-up that works by itself, capped flexible tubes at 15 V, and the code returned "crossed intact". It then flew that same flight three times, one crossing each, and reported three crossings by adding them up. It never asked for a longer flight, which was allowed and would have shown whether the C60 really oscillates. One run per version: an observation, not a benchmark.

How it was computed

  • Geometry. Each (10,10) tube is closed by half a C240 fullerene, joined ring to ring and relaxed with AIREBO. Every atom has three neighbours except the 20 at the open mouth.
  • Charges. Method of moments with Gaussian charges: the tubes are held at +V/2 and −V/2. While the C60 is within Å of a tube it sits at that tube's potential; once clear, it is an isolated conductor that keeps its total charge.
  • Dynamics. LAMMPS, AIREBO with van der Waals plus Coulomb, 1 fs steps. All charges are recomputed every fs from the current positions.
  • Tubes. Free to vibrate and deform, held only by a two-cell ring at mid-length. Against perfectly rigid caps the C60 was destroyed on impact at every voltage tried.
  • Threshold. Static estimate: the voltage at which the electrostatic pull exceeds the van der Waals retention along the whole exit path, for a C60 starting at the mouth.

What this model does not show

  • One run per result, one voltage, started at 0 K. No statistics.
  • No field emission. The C60 carries more than one electron charge in a field near 1 V/Å; a real system would leak current before that.
  • Contact is a distance threshold, not a tunnelling model, and charge transfer is instantaneous.
  • The C60 speeds up from cycle to cycle. This may be energy fed by the voltage source at each transfer, or an effect of the update interval. Not resolved.
  • No trapping. With the voltage left on, the C60 never settles in one tube; it shuttles, then escapes.
  • Known artefact: deep inside a tube the C60 should be neutral (Faraday cage); with Gaussian charges it keeps a small charge of the opposite sign.
